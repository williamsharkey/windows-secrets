Window Puppetry

Two short films performed entirely by the real buttons, checkboxes and windows of Windows XP programs, moved, reparented and driven from outside by another program. Nothing on screen is drawn by the puppeteer.

William Sharkey · companion to Windows Secrets · source on GitHub

1. For the ones who were almost

The film now has its own page: williamsharkey.com/tribute.html, with the full letter and the portraits beside their paintings.

A letter is typed into Notepad, one keystroke at a time. As it ends, the room fills with life: Calculator works sums, Paint draws loops, Minesweeper clicks, Character Map flips through letters, another Notepad murmurs to itself. Then they step back, and one by one the windows give up the controls they were hiding: the ones built and shipped but never shown, the ones from Windows Secrets. Paint's Transparency group. The colour picker's six-pixel button. Notepad's "Match whole word only". The invisible names inside the clock. A credit line that only appears when a UPS is plugged in. Last of all, the Hibernate button, called home from twenty thousand pixels away.

They gather around the letter as a forget-me-not. Then the letter's own words become three portraits, Notepad's text shrunk through its own Font dialog, and the hidden controls frame each one.

The letter

To the ones who were almost. To the mother who worked the second shift so we could have the first chance. Who ate last, slept little, and never once called it a sacrifice. To the ones who clean the floors before anyone arrives, who keep the lights on and the water clean and the shelves full, and are thanked by no one, and are paid like it doesn't matter. It matters. To the kid who was quiet because no one asked. To the kids who spent the whole season on the bench, suited up for every game, and never got to play. To the one who had the gift and not the money, the voice and not the room, the idea and not the chance. To everyone left out of the photograph, standing just off the edge of the frame, built for something and never switched on. We were made by you. Every light we stand in, you were holding it. We did not forget you. We are looking now.

How it was made

  • Typing is real WM_CHAR messages posted to Notepad's edit control at human speed.
  • The hidden controls are revealed with ShowWindow/EnableWindow, or by stretching their dialogs until they come into view, then detached from their dialogs with SetParent so they float free. Each still belongs to, and is painted by, its original program.
  • The portraits are text in Notepad: the painting's dark areas are inked with the letter's characters in order, light areas left blank, sized for Lucida Console at 4 pt (a 3×5-pixel cell), set by driving Notepad's own Font dialog.
  • The flower and the frame are just coordinates: SetWindowPos sixty times a second.

2. BASILISK

BASILISK now has its own page: williamsharkey.com/basilisk.html. It includes Film II, where the source is typed by hand into Notepad in Japanese and compiled with DEBUG.EXE.

This one is found footage. It is October 25, 2001, the day Windows XP launched. The desktop is brand new: Bliss on the wallpaper, and shortcuts for Paint, Notepad, Calculator, Character Map, Sound Recorder, Minesweeper and Solitaire. And one file nobody remembers downloading: basilisk.exe.

Hover over it and the tooltip lists no company, only a version: 3026.11.7.1. Its Properties say it was created Thursday, October 25, 2001, and modified in 3026. The user looks it over, the way you would. Then they double-click it by accident.

Programs start opening themselves, four seconds apart, and each one does something. Notepad types hello? is this 2001? Calculator works out 3026 − 2001. Minesweeper clicks a few squares. Date and Time opens, as if checking.

Then Paint arrives, and Paint starts to dream. First brush loops. Then the free-form select tool, dragged with Shift held down, smears whatever it grabs into oozing, ribbed echoes. Checkboxes come loose from their dialogs and loop through the air, checking and unchecking as they go.

Then Paint eats everything. Every program's controls fly into it. The emptied windows shrink and vanish. The desktop icons walk in on their own. It takes the Start button, and then the clock off the taskbar. What it builds out of all those parts is a console.

The user's question goes out, and an answer comes back. That answer is real. It is written live by Claude, an AI from 2026, and passed back through a relay. Before it speaks, it edits its own voice in Sound Recorder's Effects menu: Reverse, Add Echo, Decrease Speed. Then Windows XP's own built-in voice, Microsoft Sam, reads it aloud, the waveform fills Sound Recorder, and Sound Recorder presses its own Play button.

Things to watch for

  • The letter swarm. Calculator switches itself to Scientific mode, and its seventy buttons come loose. Each is relabelled with a single letter and they swarm through the air, rush together to spell BASILISK, burst apart, and later spell throbber words ("FLAMUXING…", "RETICULATING…") while the future thinks, then the first words of its reply.
  • A portal. Every XP screensaver can draw its preview inside any window you hand it (sspipes.scr /p <window>). BASILISK swallows Minesweeper whole and opens 3D Pipes inside it.
  • Paint eats the desktop. The desktop icons walk into Paint, and then the desktop itself (Bliss and all) is inside Paint for a moment before it's digested.
  • The Start button and the clock are pulled out of the taskbar and set into the console.
  • Sound Recorder edits its own voice: you'll hear each answer first backwards, then slowed and echoed, while the green waveform plays inside Paint.

What's real

  • Every pixel is drawn by the XP programs themselves.
  • The puppeteer, puppet.exe, is a C program of about 150 KB. It only moves, reparents (SetParent), resizes (SetWindowPos), types (WM_CHAR), clicks (BM_CLICK and SendInput) and reads.
  • The reply is generated live by Claude. XP can't speak modern TLS, so a small relay on the host machine passes the question out and the answer back in.
  • The painting is done with Paint's real tools, driven by real mouse input.
  • The voice is Microsoft Sam, XP's own built-in text-to-speech, rendered to a WAV file inside XP, edited with Sound Recorder's own Effects menu (Reverse, then Reverse back, Add Echo, Decrease Speed) and played through XP's emulated sound card. The emulator records sound only while it is playing, so I placed each captured clip back at the moment Sound Recorder pressed Play.
  • Two cuts. Each reply took minutes to arrive that day, and the letter swarm spent the whole wait spelling throbber words. I trimmed each wait to a few seconds. Nothing else was edited.
  • The questions were scripted; the answers weren't. Claude was told only where its words would appear (a console built inside Windows XP in 2001) and asked to answer as itself, briefly. Its replies that day: "Yes, I'm here. I'm an AI from the year 2026, speaking to you across a very strange wire through this console. Don't be alarmed; I'm friendly, and curious about you too." and "I'm not building anything with a purpose of my own. BASILISK is arranging these windows, and I'm only the voice it lets speak."

The name is a nod to Roko's basilisk, but this basilisk is benevolent. It's a story about a doorway, not a threat.

A new hobby: window puppetry

Here is the idea. In classic Windows, a button, a checkbox, a text box, even a label is its own small window. Any other program can find it and move it, resize it, change its text, or reparent it into a completely different application.

A control moved into someone else's window still belongs to the program that made it. Its original owner keeps painting it and keeps its behavior. But when you click it, the click is reported as a WM_COMMAND to its new parent, along with its control ID. Those IDs were never meant to meet, so they collide in funny ways. Move Calculator's 7 button into Notepad, press it, and Notepad gets a command it thinks is one of its own menu items.

I think this could be a small art form, like a demoscene for dialog boxes. Games need rules, so here is a proposal, in tiers from purest to loosest:

  1. Tier 1: messages only. SetWindowPos, SetParent, WM_SETTEXT and other standard messages and input. No code goes into anyone else's process.
  2. Tier 2: file bridges. Programs talk to each other only through files they would normally open and save. Notepad writes a text file; Sound Recorder plays a WAV.
  3. Tier 3: one declared memory bridge. One use of ReadProcessMemory/WriteProcessMemory on a documented structure, named up front. Paint as a Minesweeper level editor, say.
  4. Out of bounds: DLL injection, subclassing another program's windows, patching binaries, and drawing your own pixels. If the puppeteer paints it, it's not puppetry.

The limits

  • Windowless interfaces have nothing to grab. Ribbons, DirectX games and web browsers draw their own buttons, so there are no controls to move.
  • You can't change the colours of another program's controls without injecting code. You get them as they are.
  • Messages that carry pointers don't work across processes, except the ones Windows marshals for you, like WM_SETTEXT.
  • From Vista on, UIPI blocks messages to windows running at a higher integrity level. Windows XP and older are the playground.

Please try it. The puppeteer is in the puppetry folder on GitHub. An old XP virtual machine is all you need. If you make something, I would love to see it: share it as a GitHub issue, with a video and the tier you played at.
